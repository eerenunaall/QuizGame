# TV quiz show and quiz video game formats adapted for a host-led café quiz night (paper answers + TV slides + host tablet marking grid)

> **How these notes were made.** In this sandbox, direct page fetches failed for wikipedia.org, wikiwand and every Wikipedia mirror tried (DNS was blocked). Only web search worked. So every "Cited Finding" below comes from the **search-engine summary/snippet of the linked page**. I did not read the full page. Rules for these shows change between countries, channels and series, and the sources say so. The figures here are the ones the snippets agreed on. Where they conflict, both are given. One search result page for The Chase contained an embedded prompt-injection instruction. I ignored it, and no claim relies on it.
>
> **Product constraint assumed throughout:** the TV shows the question (and multiple-choice options if any). Teams of 2–6 write on paper. Sheets are swapped or collected and marked. The host enters ticks or values into a tablet marking grid, and the app computes scores. No phones, and no buzzers unless stated.
>
> **Legend for "Needs":** [PAPER] = pure paper, works with sheet-swap marking · [VALUE] = paper but the host enters a *value* (a number, an answer frequency or a wager), not just a tick, and the app computes · [BUZZER] = needs a buzzer / first-to-respond mechanism (or a host-run "hands up" substitute) · [TIMER] = needs a visible TV timer.
>

## 1. Format catalogue: original mechanic → paper-and-TV adaptation

### Takeaway
Most TV formats split into two parts: a **scoring idea**, which ports easily to paper, and a **buzzer/speed layer**, which does not. Pointless-style obscurity scoring, Only Connect clue ladders and walls, Jeopardy-style value boards and wagers, Countdown letters/numbers, closest-number and survey rounds all work on paper. The host enters a value and the tablet computes. Buzzer-race formats (University Challenge starters, Missing Vowels, The Chase cash builder, Tipping Point, Eggheads head-to-heads, Family Fortunes face-off) need re-engineering into "everyone writes, then reveal" versions or a hands-up host ritual.

### Cited Findings

**Pointless (BBC)**
- Before each show, 100 people are given 100 seconds to give as many answers as they can to each question. A correct answer scores the number of those 100 people who gave it, so obscure answers score low. An answer nobody gave scores 0 (a "pointless answer"). An incorrect answer scores 100. Lowest score is best — [Pointless, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointless); [Plus Magazine, "Pointless maths"](https://plus.maths.org/content/pointless-maths-tv-gameshows)
- In elimination rounds each round is scored on its own, and the highest-scoring team is eliminated. A tie for highest is settled by a "lockdown" tie-break using the round's last question under the same scoring — [Pointless, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointless)
- Head-to-head format and prize amounts changed between series and versions. The Australian version had different rules — [Pointless, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointless); [Pointless (Australian game show), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointless_(Australian_game_show))
- A real quiz night (Hull) used a simplified Pointless-style round: each team has one guess at a Pointless answer, and a pointless answer is worth 5 points — [Hull quiz pack](https://hullculture-prod.hull.cdl.cosector.com/downloads/vh53wv90n)

**Only Connect (BBC Four/Two)**
- The rounds are Connections, Sequences, Connecting Wall and Missing Vowels — [Only Connect, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Only_Connect)
- Connections: points fall as clues are revealed. 5 points from one clue, 3 from two, 2 from three, 1 from all four — [Only Connect, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Only_Connect); [h2g2, "Only Connect"](https://h2g2.com/edited_entry/A87945691)
- Connecting Wall: 16 clues hold four groups of four. Walls are designed with red herrings (clues that seem to fit more than one group), but only one solution is perfect. Once two groups are found, teams get only three chances to identify the remaining two before the wall freezes — [Only Connect, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Only_Connect); [leancrew, "Connections strategy"](https://leancrew.com/all-this/2023/09/connections-strategy/)
- Missing Vowels is the final buzzer round, played on word puzzles with the vowels removed. One quiz site says teams lose a point for an incorrect buzz, so scores can swing sharply. I found only that one source for the penalty — [FunTrivia, Only Connect quiz](https://funtrivia.com/quiz/television/only-connect-362688.html)
- Pub quizzes already run "what's the secret connection?" rounds, and hosts discuss them as a staple — [Ask MetaFilter, "Ideas for What's the connection pub quiz rounds"](https://ask.metafilter.com/356851/Ideas-for-Whats-the-connection-pub-quiz-rounds)
- NYT *Connections* is a close relative of the wall, and the programme itself has commented on the similarity. Players will recognise the mechanic — [Only Connect, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Only_Connect)

**The Chase (ITV and international versions)**
- Cash Builder: each contestant answers questions solo for one minute, earning money per correct answer. The Australian version pays $2,000 per correct answer — [The Chase (British), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chase_(British_game_show)); [The Chase Australia, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chase_Australia)
- Head-to-head: the contestant chooses an offer from the Chaser. The standard choice starts three steps ahead and plays for the Cash Builder total. A higher offer starts closer to the Chaser for more money, and a lower offer starts further away for less (the lower offer can be zero or even negative). On the US version, the player who takes the three-step head start needs five correct answers to get home. The UK board has seven steps — [The Chase (American), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chase_(American_game_show)); [The Chase (British), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chase_(British_game_show))
- Final Chase: surviving players get a head start of one step per player in the Final Chase (UK), and a shared prize if the Chaser fails to catch them. If all three lose their head-to-heads, the team picks one member to play the Final Chase alone — [The Chase (British), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chase_(British_game_show)); [The Chase (American), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chase_(American_game_show))

**Who Wants to Be a Millionaire? (UK)**
- In the original format, £1,000 and £32,000 were guaranteed levels ("safe havens"). If a player answers wrongly above one of these levels, the prize drops to the previous guaranteed amount — [Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?, Wikipedia mirror](https://samplecontents.library.ph/wikipedia/wp/w/Who_Wants_to_Be_a_Millionaire%253F.htm)
- The money tree was revised several times. A 2007 change made £50,000 the second safe haven, and the clock format (around 2010) used a 12-question tree. The revival reinstated the original 15-question tree. In recent series, contestants commonly set their own safety net (e.g. £32,000 or £16,000, per a fan-wiki episode log) — [Wikipedia mirror](https://samplecontents.library.ph/wikipedia/wp/w/Who_Wants_to_Be_a_Millionaire%253F.htm); [Millionaire fandom, Series 38 (UK)](https://millionaire.fandom.com/wiki/Series_38_(UK))

**Family Fortunes / Family Feud**
- Face-off: one member from each family buzzes. If the first answer is the most popular, that family wins the face-off immediately. In the US format the face-off winner chooses to play or pass, though in 1988–95 the winner automatically took control — [Family Feud, Wikipedia](https://Wikipedia.com/wiki/Family_Feud); [Family Fortunes, Wikipedia (IPFS mirror)](https://www.chise.org/ipfs/QmXoypizjW3WknFiJnKLwHCnL72vedxjQkDDP1mXWo6uco/wiki/Family_Fortunes.html)
- Strikes and steal: wrong or unlisted answers earn strikes, and three strikes lose control. On the British show the opposing family then gives one answer. If it is on the board they "steal" the round's points. If not, the original family keeps them — [Family Fortunes, Wikipedia (IPFS mirror)](https://www.chise.org/ipfs/QmXoypizjW3WknFiJnKLwHCnL72vedxjQkDDP1mXWo6uco/wiki/Family_Fortunes.html)
- Big Money: the first family past £300 (£200 in series 1) plays it. Reaching 200 points wins the top prize (£10,000). From 1994, finding all five top answers won £30,000. The 2006 revival added double-money rounds before Big Money — [Family Fortunes, Wikipedia (IPFS mirror)](https://www.chise.org/ipfs/QmXoypizjW3WknFiJnKLwHCnL72vedxjQkDDP1mXWo6uco/wiki/Family_Fortunes.html); [Family Fortunes (Irish), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_Fortunes_(Irish_game_show))

**Tipping Point (ITV)**
- A coin-pusher machine game. Counters are won by answering questions and dropped into one of four drop zones. In the final, the winner drops a gold jackpot counter and tries to push it into the win zone for £10,000. Other counters falling during the final are worth £50 each. As of Series 8, double counters falling with the jackpot double it (£20,000 or £40,000). If the categories run out, the player can take what they have or trade it for three last counters — [Tipping Point, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tipping_Point_(game_show)); [Tipping Point Australia, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tipping_Point_Australia)

**Eggheads (BBC, later Channel 5)**
- A challenger team plays a resident team of quiz champions. The first rounds are one-on-one head-to-heads, each on one subject category. Each winner earns a place in the final and each loser is eliminated. The final round is general knowledge, and surviving team members may confer. The Channel 5 era used four players per team, so there are fewer head-to-heads — [Eggheads, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eggheads_(game_show)); [Britquiz Wiki, Eggheads](https://britquiz.fandom.com/wiki/Eggheads)
- In the spin-off *Are You an Egghead?*, winning a head-to-head earned a point toward the final instead of an elimination — [Are You an Egghead?, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Are_You_an_Egghead%3F)

**University Challenge**
- Starters ("starter for ten") are answered individually on the buzzer with no conferring, for 10 points. A correct starter earns the team three bonus questions worth 5 points each, on which the team confers and the captain answers. A wrong starter passes to the other team. An incorrect early buzz (before the question is finished) costs 5 points. On ITV the 5 points went to the opponents. On the BBC they are deducted from the interrupting team — [The rules of University Challenge](https://blanchflower.org/uc/show_rules.html); [QBWiki, University Challenge](https://qbwiki.com/wiki/University_Challenge); [TV Tropes, University Challenge](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Series/UniversityChallenge)
- Picture and music starters exist. If one is missed, its bonuses are held back until the next normal starter is answered — [QBWiki, University Challenge](https://qbwiki.com/wiki/University_Challenge)

**Jeopardy!**
- Daily Double: only the player who finds it may answer. The minimum wager is $5. The maximum is the greater of the player's score and the round's top clue value ($1,000 in round 1, $2,000 in Double Jeopardy!). Right adds the wager and wrong subtracts it, so scores can go negative — [Playfactile, "How to Play Jeopardy"](https://www.playfactile.com/blog/how-to-play-jeopardy-complete-game-rules-explained/); [Weekend.com, "Jeopardy! Rules Explained"](https://www.weekend.com/post/jeopardy-rules)
- Final Jeopardy!: only players with a positive score take part. Each secretly wagers $0 up to their whole score *before* the clue is revealed. Players at $0 or below are eliminated — [Playfactile](https://www.playfactile.com/blog/how-to-play-jeopardy-complete-game-rules-explained/); [Jeopardy! History Wiki](https://jeopardyhistory.fandom.com/)

**Countdown (Channel 4)**
- The current game has 15 rounds: 10 letters rounds, 4 numbers rounds and a final conundrum. The original 9-round format ran through Series 45 — [apterous wiki, Countdown](https://wiki.apterous.org/Countdown); [apterous wiki, 9-round era](https://wiki.apterous.org/9-round_era)
- Letters: from nine letters, the longest valid word scores 1 point per letter, and a nine-letter word scores 18. Numbers: combine six numbers to reach a random three-digit target. Exact scores 10, 1–5 away 7, 6–10 away 5, more than 10 away 0. Conundrum: a nine-letter anagram on the buzzer, first correct answer 10 points, one guess each — [incoherency.co.uk, Numbers game](https://incoherency.co.uk/countdown/pages/numbers-game); [Drumond Park, Countdown board game rules](https://drumondpark.co.uk/rules/countdown); [apterous wiki](https://wiki.apterous.org/Countdown)

**Knowledge is Power (Sony PlayLink, 2017; sequel *Decades*)**
- In each regular round, players choose from three "power plays" before the question is shown, then answer. The finale offers only one. Power plays are traps put on opponents' answer screens. Gloop must be rubbed off to reveal the answers, Freeze encases answers in ice that must be tapped to break, and there are bombs and "nibblers" — [Push Square review](https://www.pushsquare.com/reviews/ps4/knowledge_is_power); [GamingTrend review](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/friend-turned-foe-show-what-you-know-knowledge-is-power-review); [Worthplaying review](https://worthplaying.com/article/2017/12/16/reviews/106536-ps4-review-knowledge-is-power/)
- Final race: players answer as fast as possible to climb the "Pyramid of Knowledge". Reviewers note that players in last place can still pull it back if they answer quickly enough — [New Game Network review](https://www.newgamenetwork.com/article/1813/knowledge-is-power-review); [Push Square review](https://www.pushsquare.com/reviews/ps4/knowledge_is_power)
- Reviewers called it "a party game first, trivia game second", with "a bit too much waiting and not enough actual quizzing" — [New Game Network](https://www.newgamenetwork.com/article/1813/knowledge-is-power-review); [Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/reviews/ps4/knowledge_is_power)
- *Decades* splits rounds into decades (sources describe roughly 12 rounds of 9 questions over three decades, plus two random bonus rounds and a finale) — [God is a Geek, Decades review](https://www.godisageek.com/reviews/knowledge-power-decades-review-sony-ps4-playlink/)

**Trivia Crack (Etermax)**
- A wheel with six category sections plus a Crown section. Answering three in a row (the crown gauge) or landing on the Crown lets you either answer for a character (one per category) or **challenge** the opponent. In a challenge you stake one of your characters against one of theirs, both answer the same six questions (one per category), and most correct wins. A tie goes to a bonus question. The first to collect all six characters wins. If nobody does by round 25, most characters wins. There are extra spins to re-roll a category — [GameSkinny review](https://www.gameskinny.com/reviews/review-trivia-crack-will-challenge-your-knowledge-and-friendships/); [AppleVis, Trivia Crack](https://applevis.com/comment/20587); [Trivia Crack, Wikipedia (IPFS mirror)](https://ipfs.aleph.im/ipfs/QmXoypizjW3WknFiJnKLwHCnL72vedxjQkDDP1mXWo6uco/wiki/Trivia_Crack.html)
- Sources disagree on whether each correct answer fills one slot of the crown gauge or one of three segments — same sources as above

**Higher or Lower (Play Your Cards Right / Card Sharks)**
- Players call "higher" or "lower" on the next card in a row. Before the first call they may change the base card. After a correct call, "change" is replaced by "freeze", which locks progress so a later miss cannot undo it. A matching card counts as a loss — [Play Your Cards Right, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_Your_Cards_Right); [Drumond Park, PYCR rules](https://drumondpark.co.uk/rules/playyourcardsright.html)

**Closest-number / estimation, jokers (pub-quiz practice)**
- Standard pub tie-break: tied teams answer one numerical question, and the closest wins. Hosts differ between plain nearest (Trivia Mafia, Watercooler) and "Price is Right" not-going-over. Watercooler breaks a further tie by first submission — [Trivia Mafia host handbook, Breaking ties](https://wiki.triviamafia.com/books/trivia-mafia-host-handbook/page/breaking-ties); [Watercooler Trivia, tiebreakers](https://watercoolertrivia.com/help-questions/how-do-tiebreakers-work); [Power Pub Quiz, tiebreakers](https://www.power-pubquiz.com/pages/tiebreaker-questions-and-answers-what-to-do-in-the-event-of-a-tie-in-your-pub-quiz)
- Suggested tie-breakers include unknowable numbers (e.g. the height of a local building in metres). Another rulebook uses sudden death, where a team that gets a tie-break question wrong is eliminated — [Power Pub Quiz](https://www.power-pubquiz.com/pages/tiebreaker-questions-and-answers-what-to-do-in-the-event-of-a-tie-in-your-pub-quiz); [RulesPal, Pub trivia rulebook](https://rulespal.com/pub-trivia/rulebook)
- SpeedQuizzing's "Nearest Wins": only one team scores per question, the one nearest the true value — [SpeedQuizzing, Nearest Wins](https://www.speedquizzing.com/?p=49)
- Joker: a team doubles its score on one round it picks, usually declared before the round (or before the quiz) by handing over a card or ticking a box on the answer sheet. Some rounds may be excluded. One commercial game's variant makes correct answers double and wrong ones −1 — [Marie Curie Christmas quiz joker cards](https://www.mariecurie.org.uk/document/christmas-quiz-joker-cards-small); [Ridley's Games Room Pub Quiz](https://boardgamematcher.com/game/ridleys-games-room-pub-quiz)

### Inferences (concrete paper-and-TV adaptations; these are design proposals, not sourced facts)

Time estimates below are my own planning figures for a café room of about 6–15 teams. They include reading the question aloud, writing time and a short reveal, but not sheet-swap marking, which adds about 2–4 min per round.

**A. Pointless round ("Least popular wins")** [VALUE][PAPER]
- *TV:* the question plus the category ("Name any country ending in -IA"). After answers are collected, the TV reveals answers one by one with their score counting down from 100 (the signature "pointless countdown" bar). Wrong = 100.
- *Teams write:* one answer per question (5 questions per round).
- *Marking:* sheets are collected rather than swapped, because the host must look up each answer's score. The tablet grid has a dropdown or autocomplete of pre-scored valid answers for that question. The host picks the matching answer and the app fills in the score, with unlisted-but-valid answers scoring 0. Invalid or blank scores 100.
- *Points:* lowest is best, which conflicts with a "highest total wins" night. Convert per question: `points = round((100 − pointless_score)/10)`, so a pointless answer gives 10 and a wrong answer 0. Optionally add a +5 bonus for a true 0 (cf. Hull's 5 points per pointless answer).
- *Survey source:* the café can't run a 100-person survey. Options: (a) a pre-built bank with frequencies from a survey the product runs once; (b) **a room-as-survey variant**: score = number of *other teams in the room* who gave the same answer (scaled to 0–100). (b) is self-contained, but needs every answer typed in by the host, which takes about 1 min per question with 10 teams.
- *Fit 2–6:* excellent. A larger team brings more obscure knowledge but the argument is "safe vs obscure", which is fun at any size.
- *Time:* about 60–90 s per question, 6–8 min per 5-question round plus a slow, dramatic reveal (about 4 min).
- *Excitement:* the reveal countdown, and the reversal of "be right" into "be right and weird". Lowest-score-wins also rewards knowledge that differs from the crowd's.

**B. Only Connect: Connections (clue ladder)** [PAPER][TIMER]
- *Original:* four clues revealed one at a time, 5/3/2/1.
- *Paper adaptation:* the TV reveals clue 1, a 20–30 s timer runs, then clue 2, and so on. Teams write their answer on a grid with four boxes per question (one per clue stage) and may write in **one box only**, the stage at which they commit. The host reveals the link, teams swap sheets, and the marker ticks the box. The tablet only needs "correct at stage k" (or the app infers points from the column), giving 5/3/2/1.
- *Integrity issue:* on paper a team could write late in an earlier box. Mitigations: different coloured pens per stage, a "pens down / fold line" ritual, or collecting a strip per stage. The simplest reliable option is **tear-off strips**: each stage has a numbered strip, handed in when committing. This is a known weakness. See Gaps.
- *Fit 2–6:* very good. Lateral thinking benefits from discussion.
- *Time:* about 2 min per connection, 4 connections in about 8–10 min.
- *Excitement:* the risk/reward of committing early. Room-wide groans as clue 2 makes it obvious.

**C. Only Connect: Sequences** [PAPER]
- *Paper:* the TV shows three items of a sequence (revealed progressively as in B). Teams write the 4th. Same 5/3/2/1-style ladder, or flat points. Marking is a tick.
- *Time:* about 2 min each.

**D. Connecting Wall** [PAPER][TIMER]
- *Original:* 16 clues, four groups, red herrings, three lives after two groups are found, timed.
- *Paper:* the TV shows a static 4×4 grid of 16 clues for 2–3 min. Teams write four groups of four (clue numbers 1–16 to keep it fast) plus the link for each group. Lives can't be enforced on paper, so drop them and score correct groups and links instead. A suggested scheme (mirroring the TV show, though not verified in my sources): 1 point per correct group, 1 per correct link, +2 bonus for all eight, max 10.
- *Marking:* swap sheets. The tablet needs 8 ticks per team, or the host enters the groups as clue numbers and the app auto-checks set equality. Numbering lets the app verify, which reduces marking errors.
- *Fit 2–6:* excellent. It's a natural group-discussion puzzle, recognisable from NYT Connections.
- *Time:* 3 min to solve plus 2 min reveal, about 5–6 min for one wall.
- *Excitement:* red herrings, the "aha" cascade, and an animated TV reveal of groups sliding together.

**E. Missing Vowels** [BUZZER] → [PAPER][TIMER] re-engineered
- *Original:* buzzer, +1/−1 (per one source).
- *Paper:* the TV shows a category ("UK seaside towns") then a rapid series of vowel-less strings ("BRGHTN", "SKGNSS"), each for 8–10 s. Teams write as many as they can. +1 per correct answer, no negatives (a blank can't be penalised on paper). For tension, use an optional −1 for a wrong written answer, which makes guessing a decision.
- *Fit 2–6:* good. More eyes is faster.
- *Time:* about 10 s per item, 4 categories × 4 items in about 3–4 min. Ideal as a quick closer before the final.

**F. The Chase ("Head start / chaser")** — partly [BUZZER]
- *Original:* cash builder, head-start offers, 7-step board, final chase.
- *Paper adaptation 1, "Choose your offer" (best fit):* before a 6–8 question multiple-choice round, each team **ticks one of three offers on its sheet**: *Safe* (each correct = 1 point), *Standard* (each correct = 2, but need ≥ N correct or score 0), *Bold* (each correct = 3, need ≥ M). The host tablet records the chosen tier, the app applies the threshold, and the TV shows a chase-board animation per team (stepping down the ladder) during the reveal. This keeps the "pick your risk" moment without buzzers.
- *Paper adaptation 2, "Beat the Chaser (the host or a house team)":* the "chaser" is a target score shown on the TV (e.g. the house/expert answers the round too, or a pre-set chaser hits 80% of questions). Teams score points only if they beat the chaser's tally on the round. Excitement is high at reveal, but it feels arbitrary if the chaser score is invented.
- *Final Chase on paper:* a 2-minute rapid-fire written list ("answer as many of these 20 questions as you can in 2 min"). The team's count of correct answers is their "steps". Then the TV plays the chaser's run as an animated pursuit, with the chaser's steps pre-set or derived from the room's average. This is excellent theatre.
- *Fit 2–6:* good. *Time:* 8–10 min per round.
- *Excitement:* visible pursuit on the TV, and the self-chosen risk.

**G. Millionaire ladder** [PAPER][VALUE]
- *Original:* 15 rising-value questions, safe havens at levels 5 and 10 (£1,000/£32,000), walk away, lifelines (50:50, phone a friend, ask the audience).
- *Paper:* one sheet has 10–12 multiple-choice questions shown in ladder order on the TV (values rise: 1,2,3,5,…). Each team must **walk away by drawing a line** under a question *before* that question's answer is revealed (a "STOP" box per rung). Reveal is one rung at a time. A team keeps the highest reached rung if all are correct up to their stop, falls back to the last safe haven (rungs 4 and 8) on their first wrong answer, and gets 0 below the first haven. This needs **sequential reveal and collection**, otherwise teams can change the stop line. Simplest: answers for the whole ladder are written up front, teams mark their STOP rung on the sheet, sheets are collected, and then the TV reveals the answers rung by rung for drama. Scoring is computed by the app from ticks plus the stop rung.
- *Lifelines on paper:* each team gets one-use lifeline tokens (paper cards handed to the host). **50:50**: the TV can't show per-team options, so the host whispers or hands a pre-printed "50:50 card" for that question. **Ask the room**: allowed to look at what a neighbouring team wrote? (chaotic). **Phone a friend → "Ask the host"** for a hint. The practical version is one paper token, "50:50 on any one question", printed per question on an envelope. Marking: the tablet records token use and can deduct points.
- *Fit 2–6:* good. *Time:* 10–12 questions × 45 s = 8–10 min, plus a 4-min dramatic reveal.
- *Excitement:* stop-or-continue tension and the fall-back cliff.

**H. Family Fortunes / Feud ("Survey says")** [VALUE] (face-off is [BUZZER])
- *Paper:* the TV shows "We asked 100 people: name something you take to the beach". Teams write up to 3 answers (or exactly 1 for a purer version). The TV reveals the board one answer at a time (top answers hidden, revealed lowest-to-highest for drama). *Points:* the sum of survey counts for the team's answers that are on the board. Strikes become "each answer not on the board = a strike", and 3 strikes means the round scores 0 (if teams write 3+ answers). *Marking:* sheet swap, and the marker writes the board position (1–8) next to each matching answer. The tablet takes the positions and computes. *Survey data:* same issue as Pointless. Use a pre-surveyed bank, or a "room survey" (answers given by most teams score most). Note the room survey here rewards *conformity*, the inverse of Pointless, and pairing the two in one night is a nice contrast.
- *Big Money final on paper:* two members of a team each answer the same 5 quick survey questions separately on separate slips (no conferring), with a 200-point target. Duplicates in the second slip score 0. This works as a showcase for the top team, not as a scored round for all.
- *Fit 2–6:* excellent. *Time:* about 2 min per question including reveal, 5 questions in about 10 min.
- *Excitement:* the slow board reveal and "survey says" sounds. Low knowledge barrier.

**I. Tipping Point** — [BUZZER] + physical machine. **Not a good paper fit.**
- The core thrill is the physical coin-pusher, which can't be recreated on paper. The best partial port is a **TV-simulated "drop" animation** as the reward for a correct answer (the host enters ticks, and the TV animates counters per team falling into a team pot, with random "double counters"). That's a cosmetic randomness layer over a normal round. Optional: a final where the leader picks one of 4 "drop zones" on the TV for a jackpot that has a random chance to fall. This adds luck, so use it only for a prize or showcase and not the score. Fit: weak. Time: a normal round plus about 2 min of animation.

**J. Eggheads ("Team vs champions")** — [BUZZER] for head-to-head; [PAPER] version possible
- *Paper:* "Beat the Eggheads/house team". Each subject round has 3 multiple-choice questions. A pre-recorded "house panel" score per round is shown after teams answer (the café regulars' team, or the quiz writer's score). Teams beating or equalling the house in a round "survive" that subject, earning a bonus. The final general-knowledge round counts surviving subjects as extra lives. In practice this becomes "N rounds, each with a bonus for beating par". Fit: good. Weakness: no real opponent present. The Are You an Egghead? variant (winning a round earns a point toward the final) is the more paper-friendly logic.

**K. University Challenge ("Starter for ten + bonuses")** — starter [BUZZER]; paper variant [PAPER]
- *Original:* individual buzzer starter (10), 3 conferred bonuses (5 each), −5 for a wrong interruption.
- *Paper variant ("unlock the bonuses"):* the TV shows a harder **starter**. Every team writes an answer. Every team that gets it right unlocks the 3 bonus questions on the same theme (shown next on the TV) worth 5 each. Teams that got the starter wrong still write bonuses, but they score 0 (or 2 each, to soften). The host marks the starter cell, and the tablet ignores bonus ticks for locked teams automatically. An individual "no conferring" starter is impossible to police on paper, so drop it. *Time:* starter 45 s + 3 bonuses × 30 s, about 2.5 min per set, 4 sets in about 10 min. *Fit 2–6:* very good. The two-stage gate creates a stake.
- A buzzer version needs a host-run "hands up / slap a card" ritual. That breaks down with more than about 6 teams and is hard to adjudicate, so mark it [BUZZER].

**L. Jeopardy ("Category × value board, Daily Double, Final wager")** [VALUE][PAPER]
- *Paper "board" round:* the TV shows a 5×5 board (categories × 100–500). Since there's no buzzer, **all teams answer every clue** the host picks (the host or the leading team chooses the next cell for drama). Points equal the cell value for a correct answer and 0 for wrong. (Optional −value for wrong makes guessing a decision, but it's harsh for casual cafés.) 25 clues take too long, so use a 4×4 or 5×3.
- *Daily Double on paper:* one hidden cell. When it's revealed, each team **writes a wager** in the "DD wager" box (0 to max(current score, top cell value)) *before* the clue appears. The host enters the wager value and a tick. The app adds or subtracts. This is excellent for catching up.
- *Final Jeopardy on paper:* one category shown, then each team writes a wager (0 to its total) on a slip and it's collected, then the clue. The answer is written on the same slip. The TV reveals in order from last place to first. This is the strongest paper-native final (see Q3).
- *Marking:* the tablet grid needs a numeric wager column. The app must validate wager ≤ allowed max (flag if exceeded).
- *Time:* about 40 s per clue, a 12-clue board in 10–12 min. Final: about 4–5 min.
- *Fit 2–6:* excellent. Wager discussion is a great group moment.

**M. Countdown: letters, numbers, conundrum** [PAPER][TIMER]
- *Letters:* the TV shows 9 letters. 30 s (Countdown's clock is famously 30 s, though not confirmed by my snippet sources). Teams write their longest word. Score = length, 18 for nine letters. *Marking:* swap sheets, and the marker writes the length (or 0 if invalid). Validity disputes need a dictionary rule. The product could include a word list so the host types the word and the app validates and scores.
- *Numbers:* the TV shows 6 numbers plus a target, 30 s. Teams write the result and the method. 10 exact / 7 within 5 / 5 within 10. The host enters the claimed result and ticks "method valid". The app computes the band. Checking arithmetic is slow and error-prone at about 30 s per sheet, so a practical option is to require the method and let the swap-marker verify it.
- *Conundrum:* the TV shows a scrambled 9-letter word. First correct wins on TV. On paper, everyone who solves it within 30 s scores 10. Or as a tie-break: write the time from the TV's running clock next to the answer, and the fastest correct wins (honour system, or the host collects slips in order of hand-in).
- *Fit 2–6:* good, though word-game skill concentrates in one person. Numbers favour the table's maths person. *Time:* about 1 min per letters/numbers game. A 6-game round (4 letters, 2 numbers) runs about 8 min.

**N. Knowledge is Power ("power plays on opponents", final race)** — the original is phone-based. Paper port [PAPER][VALUE]
- *Original:* before each question, each player secretly chooses a sabotage (gloop, freeze, bombs, nibblers) that physically hinders an opponent's touchscreen. The final is a speed race up a pyramid.
- *Paper port ("Sabotage cards"):* each team starts with 2–3 one-use **sabotage tokens**, played by writing the target team's name in a "SABOTAGE" box at the top of a round's sheet (collected before the round starts). Effects must be paper-enforceable: **Freeze:** the target team's first answer in the round doesn't count. **Gloop/Blackout:** the TV is shown *without* multiple-choice options for that team (impossible per-team on one TV, so not feasible). **Steal:** take 1 point from the target if you beat them on this round. **Shield:** a defensive token. The tablet records the token plays and applies the effects automatically. The TV shows "Team A has frozen Team C!" before the round, which is the social moment.
- *Rubber band:* give the lowest-placed team(s) an extra token each round. This mirrors KiP's idea that last place can recover. (KiP's exact token-allocation rules weren't found. See Gaps.)
- *Final race on paper:* "Pyramid" is a 2-min rapid-fire written list. Each correct answer is one step up the TV pyramid, and teams start at a level based on their pre-final standing (or flat for a reset). Same structure as the Final Chase in F.
- *Fit 2–6:* good. Targeting creates rivalry between tables, so keep it light-hearted in a café.
- *Pandora's Box:* I could not confirm this exists in Knowledge is Power from any source (see Gaps).

**O. Trivia Crack ("category wheel, crowns, duels")** [PAPER][VALUE]
- *Paper "Wheel round":* the TV spins a 6-category wheel plus a "Crown" segment. The question for the landed category is shown and all teams answer. Each team's sheet has a 6-icon **crown tracker**. A team that gets a category question right **circles that category's crown** (the host marks it and the tablet tracks it). The Crown segment means every team picks its own category: teams write a category letter on the sheet, and the host has to hand out per-category questions. That's impractical on a single TV, so instead Crown = "a free crown for any team answering this harder question". Points: 1 per correct, plus a 5-point bonus when a team completes all 6 crowns, or crowns convert to bonus points at the end.
- *Duel:* between two tied or adjacent teams, a 6-question mini-round (one per category) where both write answers. Most correct steals a crown (or points) from the other. Practical as a **tie-break** or a "challenge the leader" mechanic. Each team gets one challenge token per night, which it can spend to challenge the team directly above it in the standings.
- *Fit 2–6:* good. The wheel adds randomness and spectacle. *Time:* about 45 s per question including the spin animation.

**P. Picture round** [PAPER]
- The TV shows 6–12 images (one at a time or a grid). Teams write answers. 1 point each. Variants include zoomed-in or pixelated images revealed in stages (combined with the clue-ladder points of B), celebrity baby photos, logos with the text removed, and "connect the pictures". *Fit:* excellent. *Time:* about 20–30 s per image, 10 images in about 5 min. Paper handouts are common in pubs, but in this product the TV replaces them.

**Q. Music intros** [PAPER]
- The TV/speakers play 5–15 s intros. Teams write the artist and title (1 point each). Escalating version: 2 s / 5 s / 10 s with 3/2/1 points, using the same box-per-stage trick as B. *Fit:* excellent, with broad appeal. *Time:* about 30 s per track, 10 tracks in about 6 min. Note music licensing for public performance in venues (not researched here).

**R. Higher or Lower** [PAPER][TIMER] (sequential reveal)
- *Paper:* the TV shows a start value ("Population of Ankara: 5.8 M"), then a chain of 6–8 items. For each, teams circle H or L on a pre-printed strip. **Freeze rule:** teams may draw a "freeze line" after any item, which banks the points so far. Scoring is +1 per consecutive correct up to the first error, unless frozen (banked items keep). Teams fill all items before the reveal. The reveal goes item by item, which keeps the drama.
- *Marking:* the host enters the H/L pattern, or the app compares to the key automatically if the host types the sequence (e.g. "HHLHL"). The freeze line is entered as an index.
- *Fit 2–6:* very good, with a low knowledge barrier. *Time:* 3–4 min per chain.

**S. Closest number wins / estimation** [VALUE]
- *Paper:* the TV asks for a number. Teams write a number. The host types each team's number into the tablet. The app ranks by absolute error (or relative error for huge ranges) and awards e.g. 5/3/1 for 1st/2nd/3rd closest, or "Nearest Wins" (only the closest scores, per SpeedQuizzing). The app can also award for being "within X%". The TV reveals the true value with a number-line graphic showing every team's guess, which makes a great visual.
- *Fit 2–6:* excellent. Teams average opinions (wisdom-of-crowds at table level). *Time:* 45–60 s per question, plus a reveal of about 30 s.
- The standard tie-break (Q4).

**T. True/False lightning round** [PAPER][TIMER]
- The TV flashes statements every 5–8 s. Teams circle T/F on a numbered strip of 15–20. +1 each. Optionally −1 for a wrong answer, so blanks are a strategy. *Marking:* the host types the key and the swap-marker counts. Better: the host enters each team's T/F string and the app auto-scores. *Time:* 20 statements in about 2.5 min. *Fit:* good. A 50% guess floor flattens differences, so keep the value low or use negatives.

**U. "List as many as you can in 60 seconds"** [PAPER][TIMER][VALUE]
- The TV shows a category ("Countries bordering Germany", "Bond actors") and a 60–90 s visible countdown. Teams write a list. Score = count of valid items (the marker writes the count and the host enters the number). Cap the count at the list size, and penalise duplicates or invalid items optionally. Pointless twist: items fewer than X teams named score double (needs host entry of each item, so it's slow). *Fit:* very good. Bigger teams have an advantage (more brains, but one pen). *Time:* about 2 min per list, including marking about 3–4 min, since counting long lists is the slowest marking task here.

**V. Joker / double-points** [PAPER][VALUE]
- A team ticks the joker box on one round's sheet (before the round starts, or declared on the master sheet at the start). The tablet records which round and doubles it. Low effort, high strategic engagement, and an established pub practice.

## 2. Which formats translate well to paper with sheet-swap marking, and which need buzzers or phones?

### Takeaway
Formats where every team answers every question simultaneously and the marker only judges right/wrong (or writes a count or length) work directly with sheet swaps. Formats that score a *value* (survey frequency, closest number, wager, stop-rung) work on paper, but the host must type a number into the tablet grid rather than tick. Swapping sheets doesn't help much there, because the marker needs a lookup table. Formats whose core is "first to respond" (University Challenge starters, Missing Vowels, Family Fortunes face-off, The Chase cash builder, Countdown conundrum, Eggheads head-to-heads, Tipping Point) need buzzers. Knowledge is Power's sabotage is phone-native. All can be re-engineered into simultaneous written versions.

### Cited Findings
- University Challenge starters are explicitly buzzer and no-conferring, with an interruption penalty, a mechanic that only exists with a buzzer — [The rules of University Challenge](https://blanchflower.org/uc/show_rules.html); [QBWiki](https://qbwiki.com/wiki/University_Challenge)
- Only Connect's Missing Vowels is a final buzzer round — [FunTrivia](https://funtrivia.com/quiz/television/only-connect-362688.html); [Only Connect, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Only_Connect)
- Family Fortunes' face-off is decided by who buzzes first — [Family Fortunes, Wikipedia (IPFS mirror)](https://www.chise.org/ipfs/QmXoypizjW3WknFiJnKLwHCnL72vedxjQkDDP1mXWo6uco/wiki/Family_Fortunes.html)
- Countdown's conundrum is a buzzer round, one guess each — [apterous wiki](https://wiki.apterous.org/Countdown); [Drumond Park](https://drumondpark.co.uk/rules/countdown)
- Knowledge is Power's power plays are touchscreen hindrances on each player's phone (rubbing off gloop, tapping ice), so they're inherently device-based — [Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/reviews/ps4/knowledge_is_power); [GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/friend-turned-foe-show-what-you-know-knowledge-is-power-review)
- Pub quizzes already handle jokers on paper by a tick box on the answer sheet or a card handed to the host — [Marie Curie joker cards](https://www.mariecurie.org.uk/document/christmas-quiz-joker-cards-small)
- Closest-number tiebreakers are a standard paper/pub mechanic — [Trivia Mafia](https://wiki.triviamafia.com/books/trivia-mafia-host-handbook/page/breaking-ties); [Power Pub Quiz](https://www.power-pubquiz.com/pages/tiebreaker-questions-and-answers-what-to-do-in-the-event-of-a-tie-in-your-pub-quiz)

- **Sheet-swap pitfalls:** value-scored formats (Pointless, survey, closest) can't be peer-marked reliably, because the marker needs the answer table. The product should show the answer table on the TV during marking (fine for a survey board, since it is the reveal), or have the host type the values. Closest-number needs the raw number typed by the host for every team. With 10 teams that's about 1 min per question.
- **Host-tablet grid implications:** the grid needs typed cells beyond a boolean. Stage-index (ladder), numeric value (closest, length, count, wager), enum/autocomplete (Pointless/survey answer), sequence string (H/L, T/F), token/target (joker, sabotage, offer tier), stop-rung. Validation is needed for wagers (≤ max) and counts (≤ list size).
- **Buzzer substitute without phones:** "first hand up" works with about 6 teams or fewer in a small room, but it's disputed often. A fairer option is that "all correct slips handed in within T seconds score". For tie-breaks only, use the hand-in order the host observes.

## 3. Which formats help trailing teams catch up without feeling unfair?

### Takeaway
The fairest catch-up tools are **self-chosen risk**: wagers (Daily Double, Final Jeopardy), jokers and double-down rounds, Chase-style offer tiers, and Millionaire stop lines. Teams opt in, so the leader can't call it rigged. Next fairest are **variance-boosting formats** that compress skill gaps: Pointless obscurity scoring, survey rounds, closest-number, higher/lower, true/false with negatives. **Explicit rubber-banding** (giving trailers extra power plays, Knowledge-is-Power-style, or fixed head starts) works if announced up front and kept small. Unannounced or large rubber bands feel unfair.

### Cited Findings
- Final Jeopardy lets each player wager anything from $0 to their whole score, and the Daily Double lets a player at $0 or below still wager up to the round's top value. Both let trailing players make up large gaps by choosing risk — [Playfactile](https://www.playfactile.com/blog/how-to-play-jeopardy-complete-game-rules-explained/); [Weekend.com](https://www.weekend.com/post/jeopardy-rules)
- The Chase's offers let the contestant choose a closer/riskier start for more money, or a safer start for less — [The Chase (American), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chase_(American_game_show)); [The Chase (British), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chase_(British_game_show))
- Knowledge is Power reviewers note that in the final race "even those in last can pull it back" by answering quickly. The power plays are aimed at opponents, so players in the lead get targeted — [Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/reviews/ps4/knowledge_is_power); [New Game Network](https://www.newgamenetwork.com/article/1813/knowledge-is-power-review)
- Only Connect's Missing Vowels penalty "means that scores can radically change during this final section" — [FunTrivia](https://funtrivia.com/quiz/television/only-connect-362688.html)
- Pub quizzes use jokers (double one chosen round). One variant makes wrong answers −1 under the joker, which adds risk — [Marie Curie joker cards](https://www.mariecurie.org.uk/document/christmas-quiz-joker-cards-small); [Ridley's Games Room Pub Quiz](https://boardgamematcher.com/game/ridleys-games-room-pub-quiz)
- Trivia Crack's challenge lets a player stake a character to take one of the opponent's, a direct "attack the leader" swing — [GameSkinny](https://www.gameskinny.com/reviews/review-trivia-crack-will-challenge-your-knowledge-and-friendships/)
- Family Fortunes' steal lets the non-controlling family take a whole round's points with one good answer — [Family Fortunes, Wikipedia (IPFS mirror)](https://www.chise.org/ipfs/QmXoypizjW3WknFiJnKLwHCnL72vedxjQkDDP1mXWo6uco/wiki/Family_Fortunes.html)
- Some reviewers found Knowledge is Power more party game than trivia game, so chaos-heavy mechanics trade quiz integrity for fun — [New Game Network](https://www.newgamenetwork.com/article/1813/knowledge-is-power-review)

### Inferences
- **Ranked by perceived fairness (my judgement):**
  1. *Final wager (Final Jeopardy):* everyone has the same tool. It mathematically lets a trailing team win, and the leader keeps control by betting to cover. Best catch-up for a final.
  2. *Joker (double one round), declared in advance:* symmetric, and rewards self-knowledge. A trailing team can't gain much more than the leader, though. Mild.
  3. *Offer tiers (Chase), double-or-nothing, Millionaire stop line:* opt-in risk per round. Trailing teams naturally go bold.
  4. *High-variance round types* (Pointless, survey, closest, higher/lower): knowledge matters less, so the field compresses. Fair because the rules are the same for everyone, but too many make the night feel random.
  5. *Steal mechanics* (Family Fortunes steal → "any team that names a board answer the controlling team missed gets points"; Trivia Crack challenge → one challenge token per team per night against the team directly above): fun and targeted at leaders. Keep the stakes capped.
  6. *Explicit rubber band* (Knowledge-is-Power-style: last-placed team(s) get an extra sabotage or joker token, or the final starts with a head start proportional to the *inverse* of rank but capped): acceptable if announced at the start. Avoid hidden handicaps.
- **Avoid:** randomness that ignores knowledge entirely (Tipping Point drops as score), and negative-marking on casual rounds, which punishes beginners and slows marking.
- **Design rule:** catch-up should come from choices trailing teams make and the leader can respond to (wager to cover, choose to freeze), not from the system giving points to the losers.

### Gaps
- No empirical source (player studies or host surveys) on how pub players perceive the fairness of catch-up mechanics. The ranking above is a judgement call.
- Knowledge is Power's exact allocation of power plays by rank wasn't found.

## 4. Good formats for a final round and for a tie-break

### Takeaway
**Final round:** Final-Jeopardy-style secret wager on one hard question is the best paper-native final (simultaneous, suspenseful reveal from last place to first, gives trailers a real shot). Strong alternatives are a "Final Chase / Pyramid race" (2-minute rapid-fire written list animated on TV against a chaser or as a race) and a Millionaire-style ladder with a stop line. **Tie-break:** a closest-number question is the de facto pub standard (one written number, plain nearest wins). Use sudden-death written questions or a Countdown conundrum by hand-in order as a second-level breaker.

### Cited Findings
- Final Jeopardy: secret wager of $0 up to the full score, placed before the clue is revealed. Only positive scores play — [Playfactile](https://www.playfactile.com/blog/how-to-play-jeopardy-complete-game-rules-explained/)
- The Chase's Final Chase pits the surviving team (with a head start of one step per player) against the Chaser for a shared prize — [The Chase (British), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chase_(British_game_show))
- Knowledge is Power's finale is a speed race up the Pyramid of Knowledge, and the finale offers a single power play instead of three — [New Game Network](https://www.newgamenetwork.com/article/1813/knowledge-is-power-review); [Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/reviews/ps4/knowledge_is_power)
- Only Connect ends with Missing Vowels, a fast round where scores can swing — [FunTrivia](https://funtrivia.com/quiz/television/only-connect-362688.html)
- Family Fortunes ends with Big Money: two members answer five survey questions separately against a 200-point target — [Family Fortunes, Wikipedia (IPFS mirror)](https://www.chise.org/ipfs/QmXoypizjW3WknFiJnKLwHCnL72vedxjQkDDP1mXWo6uco/wiki/Family_Fortunes.html)
- Pointless resolves ties for highest with a "lockdown" question using the same scoring — [Pointless, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointless)
- Pub tie-break practice: one numerical question, closest wins. Plain nearest (Trivia Mafia, Watercooler) vs not-going-over (Price is Right). If still tied, Watercooler uses first submission. Suggested questions are unknowable numbers (e.g. the height of a local building). Some rulebooks use sudden-death elimination questions — [Trivia Mafia](https://wiki.triviamafia.com/books/trivia-mafia-host-handbook/page/breaking-ties); [Watercooler Trivia](https://watercoolertrivia.com/help-questions/how-do-tiebreakers-work); [Power Pub Quiz](https://www.power-pubquiz.com/pages/tiebreaker-questions-and-answers-what-to-do-in-the-event-of-a-tie-in-your-pub-quiz); [RulesPal](https://rulespal.com/pub-trivia/rulebook)
- Countdown's conundrum (a nine-letter anagram, first correct takes 10) is the show's own decisive closing round — [apterous wiki](https://wiki.apterous.org/Countdown); [Drumond Park](https://drumondpark.co.uk/rules/countdown)

### Inferences
**Final-round options for paper + TV (my recommendations):**

| Final format | How it runs on paper | Time | Catch-up power | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Final wager (Jeopardy)** | TV shows the category, teams write a wager (≤ their total) on a slip, collected. Clue shown, 60 s, answer on the same slip. The host enters wager + tick, and the TV reveals from last to first. | 4–6 min | High, and opt-in | Best default. The tablet must show each team its max wager (current total), which the TV can display as the standings before the wager. |
| **Final Chase / Pyramid race** | 2–3 min rapid-fire written list of 15–20 questions on the TV (auto-advancing every 8 s). Count correct = steps, with a head start of +1 step per X points of pre-final total (optional). TV animates the race or the chaser. | 5–7 min | Medium | Very watchable. Needs fast counting (the app auto-scores if the host types answers, else the marker writes the count). |
| **Millionaire ladder** | Rising values, a stop line, safe havens. Reveal rung by rung. | 8–12 min | Medium-high | Great drama but long. Good as the final for the top 3 only (showcase). |
| **Survey "Big Money"** | Top team(s) only: two members write separately against 200. | 3–4 min | n/a (showcase/prize) | Not a scoring final for everyone. |
| **Missing Vowels sprint** | Timed written sprint. | 3–4 min | Medium | Good penultimate round, less good as the decider. |

**Tie-break hierarchy (my recommendation):**
1. **Closest number** (one written number, plain nearest, ideally an unguessable but fair quantity). The host types the numbers and the app computes |error|. The TV shows a number-line reveal. About 1 min.
2. If still exactly tied: **earliest hand-in** of that slip (the host marks order), or a second closest-number question.
3. Alternative or showpiece: **Countdown conundrum by hand-in order** (the first correct slip handed to the host wins). Visible and fast, but it needs the host to adjudicate order.
4. Avoid buzzer face-offs unless the tied teams are physically near the host.
- *Product implication:* the tie-break is resolved by a numeric input in the tablet. Store the raw guesses so the TV can show them.

- The "earliest submission" tie rule is sourced only from Watercooler (an online platform), not a paper pub setting.

## 5. Rough time per question and per round (summary)

### Takeaway
Simultaneous written rounds run about 20–60 s per question (5–10 min per round), plus 2–4 min for swap-and-mark. Value-scored rounds (Pointless, survey, closest) add about 1 min per question of host data entry with around 10 teams. Progressive-reveal formats (ladders, Millionaire, Jeopardy board) cost the most time per point but give the most drama.

### Cited Findings
- On-show timings found: The Chase cash builder is one minute per player — [The Chase (British), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chase_(British_game_show)). Pointless survey respondents get 100 seconds per question — [Pointless, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointless). Push Square's KiP review observes "a bit too much waiting and not enough actual quizzing", a warning about time spent on mechanics vs questions — [Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/reviews/ps4/knowledge_is_power)

- A typical 2-hour café night (about 100 min of play after setup and breaks) fits **6–8 rounds**. For example: standard opener (10) → picture (8) → Connections/Wall (10) → music (9) → Pointless or survey (12) → Jeopardy board with Daily Double (12) → lightning or Missing Vowels (5) → final wager (5), plus tie-break if needed. That's about 70–80 min of rounds plus about 20 min of transitions and standings.
